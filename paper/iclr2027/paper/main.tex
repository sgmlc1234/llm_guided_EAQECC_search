\begin{abstract}
LLM-guided program evolution can produce mathematical constructions,
but validity, generality, and reproducibility require distinct evidence.
We develop a researcher-in-the-loop discovery pipeline that combines
program evolution, exact evaluation, mathematical generalization,
certified nonexistence checks, and formal proof checking. We apply it
to entanglement-assisted quantum error-correcting codes (EAQECCs).
Starting from tasks that did not specify a family formula, evolved
programs produced finite qubit constructions whose structure researchers
generalized to the family $[[n,1,n-1;n-q-1]]_q$ for prime powers
$q\ge3$ at $n\ge2q+1$ and for $q=2$ at odd $n\ge5$. Lean checks the
algebraic certificate underlying this family. Together with entanglement
lifting, the family closes 54 gaps in archived EAQECC tables. The
released evidence includes 115 verified code records, ten nonexistence
results with checked DRAT proofs, family implementations, and preserved
search programs for exact checks and seeded replay. In a controlled
cohort of eight paired generation blocks, validation-selected iterative
programs achieve success in 46.1\% and 31.6\% of held-out executions at
unseen lengths $n=13,15$, compared with 19.5\% and 3.1\% for independently
generated programs. In this setting, program evolution and researcher
analysis turn finite computational discoveries into a proved family
with independently checkable evidence.
\end{abstract}

%==============================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================

LLM-guided program search has produced mathematical constructions and
algorithms~\citep{FunSearch,AlphaEvolve}. Their outputs can also support
general mathematical results through researcher analysis. This raises
three questions: \emph{whether evolved programs produce valid codes,
whether their structure can be generalized and proved to work beyond
the searched cases, and whether the resulting constructions and preserved
searches can be independently reproduced}. Addressing these questions
requires records of the initial task, computational discoveries and
subsequent mathematical analysis.

We study this process for entanglement-assisted quantum error-correcting
codes (EAQECCs). Early AlphaEvolve searches produced finite qubit codes
whose structure led us to an infinite qubit family. Further mathematical
generalization gives the prime-power construction and proof in
Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}. The initial instructions suggested directions
such as cyclic shifts, but did not provide this family formula.
Later instructions explicitly incorporated the discovered qubit family
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}), allowing it to guide further searches.

Our reproducibility contribution concerns these mathematical
constructions and their computational evidence. The \artifactlink{} supplies
code matrices, family implementations, proofs, task specifications and
preserved search programs. Readers can recompute code parameters,
construct and verify family instances, inspect the proof and replay
preserved searches. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes how researcher
analysis and program evolution interact. Section~\ref{sec:repro}
specifies which operations are reproducible. A final Lean stage checks
the algebraic construction underlying Theorem~\ref{thm:unified} (Appendix~\ref{app:lean}); model generation
remains stochastic.

Several improvements concern constructions published in 2020 and 2022
that remained listed in the EAQECC tables accessed on 17 July
2026~\citep{GHW2020,Luo2022}. Our constructions and certified exclusions
close nine previously tabulated qubit distance gaps (Table~\ref{tab:results}).
The prior constructions predate our table access by approximately four
to six years. Their dates document available constructions rather than
a complete history of improvement attempts.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{A researcher-in-the-loop discovery pipeline.}
  We connect LLM-guided program evolution with researcher proposals,
  mathematical generalization and formal proof checking for EAQECC
  discovery. Preserved programs, code matrices and formal certificates
  support reconstruction and independent verification of the resulting
  constructions (Sections~\ref{sec:pipeline} and~\ref{sec:repro}).
\item \textbf{General constructions from evolved program outputs.}
  Finite qubit constructions led to the general family of
  Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}, whose algebraic construction is checked in Lean.
  The family and entanglement lifting close 54 listed gaps, while checked
  nonexistence certificates establish selected optimality and
  minimum-entanglement results (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{Program development and generalization.} We compare
  independent and iterative generation under shared research guidance.
  Eight new generation blocks yield longer-code success of 46.1\% and
  31.6\% for iterative programs, versus 19.5\% and 3.1\% for independent
  proposals at $n=13,15$. Preserved lineages identify useful objective and
  search-policy changes (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The researcher-in-the-loop discovery pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}
%==============================================================

Our discovery pipeline combines human-in-the-loop (HITL) research with
LLM-guided program evolution and exact verification. Researchers propose
construction ideas, inspect verified instances and revise the task between
campaigns. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} connects these interventions to the
automated search and evaluation loop. Constructed codes establish lower
distance bounds; SAT refutations with checked certificates lower upper
bounds. Independent verification and archived evidence support both routes.
Researchers generalize the discovered constructions and develop proofs,
then pass the formalized statements and proofs to the Lean checking stage.

\input{fig_overview}

\subsection{Target selection}

We compare our constructions with the bounds listed in the EAQECC tables
(\texttt{codetables.de}), accessed on 17 July 2026. These reference bounds
define listed-gap and absent-cell targets, filtered using the EA-Plotkin
and applicable Singleton bounds. EA-Plotkin also tightens 398 upper bounds
in the reference qubit table. Appendix~\ref{app:tables} gives the table
records and filters. The artifact preserves these comparison data and
uses them by default. A second copy, retrieved on 10 September 2026,
contains identical intervals at all 11,640 qubit and qutrit entries.

\subsection{LLM-guided program evolution}

The qubit discovery campaigns use AlphaEvolve~\citep{AlphaEvolve} through
Google Cloud Vertex AI. We provide a seed program, task specification
and exact evaluator; AlphaEvolve generates revisions and archives their
source and outcomes. An LLM modifies a designated block of the seed.
Within each candidate program, an exact parameter oracle evaluates
intermediate matrices; the program uses these results to choose targets,
accept local moves and restart search. Algorithm~\ref{alg:search}, for
example, minimizes a cost combining signature mismatch and the number
of logical operators below the target distance.

After the program returns up to 16 matrices, the external evaluator
recomputes their parameters and assigns the best proposal score to the
program. Its reward favors a valid signature, fewer low-weight logical
operators and newly achieved targets. This reward guides the next program
revision; the inner cost guides matrix search within one execution.
Independent code checking follows in Section~\ref{sec:refute}.

Each candidate runs in its own subprocess with a fixed 240\,s budget
and a specified seed for its random-number generator. Failures receive a low score, and the campaign continues.
The same interface supports fixed-program replay (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).
The four campaign logs record $401$ completed candidate searches,
corresponding to $26.7$\,h of allocated search budget
(Appendix~\ref{app:campaign}).

The initial task specification did not contain the family proved in
Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}. We started with an annealing seed and instructions suggesting cyclic
shifts among other construction directions. Evolved programs produced finite qubit codes; analysis of
their structure led researchers to a general qubit family and its proof.
The later task specifications included that family and requested further
constructions, while the best program was reused as a new seed.
These records distinguish the initial discovery stage from subsequent
search using the mathematical result (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
The current prime-power theorem extends the qubit construction through
further researcher-led generalization and proof.

\subsection{Independent verification and nonexistence checking}\label{sec:refute}

The Magma export supplies only a basis of the symplectic dual
$L=S^\perp$, where $S$ is the stabilizer space. Magma independently
recomputes the dual, radical and $(k,c,d)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:symplectic}).
All 148 objects---115 core codes at $q=2,3,4,5$ and 33 family instances---were re-verified with zero mismatches under Magma V2.28-20.
Separate $\F_2$ linear-algebra checks verify the two solver-refined
qubit codes; all code records can also be checked in Python using the
\artifactlink{}. Lean checks the algebraic certificate underlying
Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}. A separate Comparator run checks the submitted
proof against the exact formal goal and permitted axioms
(Appendix~\ref{app:lean}).


To test existence of an $[[n,k,d;c]]_q$ code, we encode the smaller
subspace $L=S^\perp$ in symplectic normal form. Constraints enforce
the target signature directly, so every satisfying assignment specifies
an EAQECC. Distance constraints dominate the encoding.

For an unsatisfiable encoding, the SAT solver produces a DRAT proof,
a certificate of unsatisfiability checked independently by
DRAT-trim~\citep{DRATtrim}. Together with the encoding's completeness,
this establishes the corresponding code nonexistence result. The qutrit encoding uses a coordinate-normalization
lemma to preserve completeness. Appendix~\ref{app:sat} gives the proof
scope and encoding, and Section~\ref{sec:optimality-results} reports the
certified results.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

Readers can reconstruct two outputs: code constructions and preserved
program executions. Supplied matrices support exact $(k,c,d)$ checks;
family implementations generate instances, and Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}
proves the construction for all admissible parameters. Task specifications
and discovery logs document the progression from finite qubit codes to
the researcher-derived family (Appendix~\ref{app:campaign}). Source replay
concerns retained programs, including later programs that use this family.
It replaces wall time with a virtual clock tied to evaluator calls and
preserves the recorded random-number seeds and stopping conditions.
Three programs with four seeds each reproduce all twelve stored proposal
lists, with agreement between one and four workers. The \artifactlink{}
provides these inputs, independent parameter checks and reference-bound
audits.

\subsection{Code families and reduced entanglement}\label{sec:family-results}

Theorem~\ref{thm:unified} is the mathematical generalization of the
construction identified through the evolved qubit codes. It gives
$[[n,1,n-1;n-q-1]]_q$ for $q\ge3$, $n\ge2q+1$, and for $q=2$
at odd $n\ge5$. The proof in Appendix~\ref{app:theorem} establishes
the entire family; the implementations verify finite instances at
$q=2,3,4,5$. Entanglement lifting increases $c$ by one at fixed $(n,k)$
without reducing distance when $c<n-k$ (Lemma~\ref{app:lifting}).
The qubit family closes 29 listed gaps. For $q=3$, lifting from $c=n-4$
to the table's $c=n-3$ closes 25 more, for 54 in total.
This family addresses finite-length distance--entanglement tradeoffs:
at fixed $q$, its rate $k/n=1/n$ vanishes and $c/n$ tends to one.
The broader archive includes 29 binary codes with $k>1$, up to $k=6$.

Additional constructions attain $d=n-1$ at $c=n-q-2$.
The displayed $q=4,5$ examples include $[[10,1,9;4]]_4$ and
$[[12,1,11;5]]_5$, requiring four and five shared pairs, respectively.
These constructions establish achievable
entanglement costs; smaller costs at $q=4,5$ have not been excluded.
Complementary finite-field annealing searches produce 13 archived codes
at $q=4$ and 10 at $q=5$ (Appendix~\ref{app:nonbinary-search}).
Table~\ref{tab:results-qudit} selects those using the least entanglement
reached at the displayed lengths.

\subsection{Upper bounds and optimality}\label{sec:optimality-results}

Solver refinement settles the two remaining qubit gaps from
Table~\ref{tab:results}. Normal-form SAT finds $[[12,2,9;9]]_2$,
improving upon an intermediate $d=8$ construction from our earlier search. EA-Plotkin gives
$d\le\lfloor144/15\rfloor=9$, so the new code is distance-optimal.
At $(n,k,c)=(13,1,8)$, a DRAT-certified refutation excludes $d\ge12$;
our existing $d=11$ code is therefore optimal. Both codes are
checked by separate binary linear-algebra code, and DRAT-trim replays
the nonexistence certificate. We attribute these two closures to solver refinement.

Matching nonexistence results also establish minimum entanglement at
selected lengths. For qubits at $k=1$, $d=n-1$, the minimum is $n-3$
at $n=7,9,11$ and $n-4$ at $n=6,8,10$. This finite-length parity
pattern combines distinct constructions with certified exclusions;
Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}'s binary branch covers odd lengths.
For qutrits, the certified
exclusion of $[[10,1,9;4]]_3$ and a code at $c=5$ give
$\cmins(10,1,9)=5$. Two further exclusions, $[[5,2,4;3]]_2$ and $[[6,2,5;4]]_2$,
were also checked by exhaustive enumeration. All ten exclusions have
checked DRAT proofs (Appendix~\ref{app:sat-resources}).

Applying the EA-Plotkin bound of \citet{GuoLi2013} additionally lowers
398 listed qubit upper bounds. The auditor recomputes these corrections
against the reference table data. These corrections follow from a known
inequality and are reported as a separate source of upper bounds.

\section{Ablation Study}\label{sec:ablation}
%==============================================================

We test whether iterative feedback helps develop a researcher-proposed
change into an effective search program. Both policies receive the same
initial code and research guidance; their difference is how they reuse
previous programs and execution feedback.

\subsection{Program generation}
The shared proposal is to maintain and mutate a basis of
$L=S^\perp$, converting it to $S$ for exact evaluation. For
$[[9,1,8;6]]_2$ and $[[11,1,10;8]]_2$, this replaces a basis of 14 or
18 generators with four vectors. Symplectic complementation preserves
the feasible code set but changes the variables and local mutations.
A preliminary calibration compares the initial $S$-side program with
a working $L$-side control and fixes a budget at which the change can
produce measurable success. Neither the control's source nor generator
matrices are supplied to the model.

\emph{Independent proposals} always receive the initial program and its
fixed training measurements. \emph{Iterative evolution} receives the
best-so-far program and the most recent attempt, including rejected
attempts, with training feedback for both. The model can revise the
representation, objective, mutation operators and restart policy.
A proposal is a whole program revision; an evaluator call checks one
candidate matrix produced by that program. Both use Gemini 3.5 Flash
and identical tool access. There is no
controller initialization: every success must be produced by the
submitted program.

An initial four-block study was followed by a prospective eight-block
replication using new generation and execution seeds. Each policy is
allocated seven proposals per block. The replication receives 111 of
112 planned responses; one iterative pool has six proposals. All eight
blocks are retained under a rule fixed before validation and testing.
Training uses two seeds and 10,000 exact evaluations per target. Four
new validation seeds select among the three training-best distinct
programs; 32 further seeds evaluate each selection. Only training
feedback enters generation. The original and new cohorts are reported
separately (Appendix~\ref{app:ablations}).

\input{fig_ablation_comparison}

In the new cohort, success at $n=9,11$ is 72.7\% and 43.4\% for
iterative evolution, versus 60.2\% and 27.0\% for independent proposals.
The prespecified primary endpoint is the paired block difference in
mean normalized success-curve area: 0.359 versus 0.260, a gain of 0.099.
Five of eight block differences are positive; a descriptive two-sided
sign-flip test gives $p=0.328$. Generation blocks, rather than execution
seeds, are the units for policy inference. Restricting to the seven
fully received paired blocks gives a gain of 0.150.

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-comparison} aggregates both lengths within each
panel. Each policy contributes eight selected programs and 32 runs per
length; each fixed control contributes 32 runs per length. Across the
two generation lengths, iterative selections solve 297/512 executions,
versus 223/512 for independent selections. Every saved successful code
passes an independent parameter check. Appendix~\ref{app:hitl-outcomes}
reports the earlier cohort and its fresh-seed repeat separately; the
artifact also preserves model-use accounting for both policies.

\subsection{Generalization to longer codes}\label{sec:longer-codes}
The selected programs are applied without further modification to
$[[13,1,12;10]]_2$ and $[[15,1,14;12]]_2$ at 30,000 calls.
These lengths are withheld from generation and selection; their outcomes
are secondary endpoints. In the new eight-block cohort, iterative
success is 118/256 (46.1\%) and 81/256 (31.6\%), versus 50/256
(19.5\%) and 8/256 (3.1\%) for independent proposals. Two iterative
selections achieve 32/32 at $n=13$ and at least 28/32 at $n=15$
(Appendix~\ref{app:replication}).
Their mean normalized curve areas are 0.309 and 0.193, compared with
0.099 and 0.012 for independent proposals.

A preserved lineage from the original cohort gives a source-level
diagnosis of one useful edit. Adding a penalty for distance below the
target---the only functional search change in that step---raises
$n=13$ success from 13/32 to 29/32 and $n=15$ success from 3/32 to
28/32. The validation-selected child (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ablation-search})
then bundles changes to objective weights, moves and restart behavior;
we therefore use the earlier step for edit-level attribution and report
the complete lineage in Appendix~\ref{app:hitl-program}. All resulting
codes pass independent linear-algebra checks. Table~\ref{tab:results}
includes the longer-code parameters alongside their prior intervals.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}\label{sec:limitations}
%==============================================================

The controlled evidence is scoped to one model, one researcher proposal
and binary targets. The eight generation blocks are the independent
units for policy inference; repeated executions measure frozen-program
variability. The policies receive equal proposal allocations but use
different token totals. Exact distance verification
enumerates symplectic duals, so its cost grows exponentially with their
dimension. The distance guarantees assume ideal decoding and noiseless
receiver-side halves; circuit costs and hardware-level error rates
are not evaluated. For these controlled studies, the artifact preserves
all generated sources, requests and selection records and supports fixed-program replay;
stochastic model sampling is documented rather than expected to return
byte-identical programs.

The central mathematical result developed from code constructions
produced by evolved programs. Researchers analyzed their structure,
derived a qubit family, and extended and proved the prime-power
construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}. Later prompts reused the
qubit family, showing how a result obtained through program search can
guide further research. The family and entanglement lifting close 54
listed gaps. Normal-form SAT completes two final qubit closures, ten
checked DRAT refutations certify selected upper bounds and minimum
entanglement costs, and EA-Plotkin tightens 398 listed upper bounds.

Supplied matrices, family implementations and independent verifiers
support reconstruction and checks of finite instances; preserved programs
support seeded replay. The proof covers every admissible parameter.
Independent Magma verification reports zero mismatches across 148
objects, and Lean checks the algebraic certificate underlying the
researcher-derived theorem. The prospective eight-block cohort identifies
useful program revisions and higher held-out longer-code success among
iterative selections under shared research guidance. Applying the
workflow to another problem still requires its own representation,
evaluator and mathematical analysis.

\section{The domain, the tables, and the targets}\label{app:domain}

\subsection{Codes as subspaces of $\F_q^{2n}$}\label{app:symplectic}
For prime-power $q$, our search uses $\F_q$-linear stabilizer EAQECCs.
Stabilizer generators are products of Pauli operators defining
error-detection constraints. Entanglement assistance permits noncommuting
generators on the transmitted qudits by completing them with operators
on the receiver's halves into commuting constraints. Their commutation
relations determine the entanglement cost~\citep{BrunDevetakHsieh2006,WildeBrun2008}.
Nonadditive EAQECCs form a different search space~\citep{Shin2011}.

We write vectors in bold, scalar coordinates and coefficients in italic,
and subspaces with italic capitals such as $S,L,R$.
Modulo phase, a Pauli operator is represented by
$\vct{u}=(x_1,z_1,\ldots,x_n,z_n)\in\F_q^{2n}$. For qubits, the pairs
$(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,1)$ represent $I,X,Z,Y$, respectively. The
symplectic product with $\vct{v}=(x'_1,z'_1,\ldots,x'_n,z'_n)$ is
\[
 \langle \vct{u},\vct{v}\rangle=\sum_i(x_i z_i'-z_i x_i'),
\]
and the symplectic weight $\wt(\vct{u})$ counts positions with
$(x_i,z_i)\ne(0,0)$. For qubits, zero symplectic product means that
the Pauli operators commute.

Let $S$ be the generator span and set $s=\dim S$. Its symplectic dual
$S^\perp$ consists of vectors orthogonal to $S$.
For any subspace $U\le\F_q^{2n}$, define its radical by
$\operatorname{rad}(U):=U\cap U^\perp$. Thus
$S_{\mathrm{iso}}=\operatorname{rad}(S)$ contains stabilizer
operations with trivial logical action. Vectors in
$S^\perp\setminus S_{\mathrm{iso}}$ represent undetectable errors
with nontrivial logical action. The code parameters are
\[
  c=\frac{s-\dim S_{\mathrm{iso}}}{2},\qquad
  k = n - s + c, \qquad
  d = \min\{\wt(\vct{v}) : \vct{v} \in S^{\perp}\setminus S_{\mathrm{iso}}\}.
\]
Finite-field linear algebra gives the dual and radical. With
$r=\dim L=n+k-c$, the dual has $q^r$ vectors, of which
$q^r-q^{n-k-c}$ are non-radical; their minimum weight gives $d$.
For Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}, $r=q+2$ is independent of length at fixed $q$.
At every binary ablation length, $r=4$: 16 dual vectors and 12
non-radical vectors. The controlled evaluator computes the radical
from the Gram kernel, avoiding enumeration of $S$. The archived
discovery helper instead enumerates both $S$ and $L$, totaling
$2^s+2^r$ vectors before filtering. Thus implementation and subspace
dimensions determine cost, independently of the search representation.
Parameters are recomputed from generators, not search-program reports.

To propagate constructions and exclusions across entanglement costs,
we use the following form of the standard rule in
\citet[Section IV, rule (4)]{Luo2022}. We include its symplectic argument
for completeness.

\begin{lemma}\label{app:lifting}
An $\F_q$-linear stabilizer EAQECC with parameters $[[n,k,d;c]]_q$,
$k\ge1$ and $c<n-k$, gives an $\F_q$-linear stabilizer EAQECC with
parameters $[[n,k,d';c+1]]_q$ and $d'\ge d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
Set $L=S^\perp$ and $R=\operatorname{rad}(L)$, with $\dim R=j=n-k-c>0$.
Choose $L=U\oplus R$. The form on $U$ is nondegenerate, since a
vector of $U$ orthogonal to $U$ also lies in $R$, hence in $U\cap R=\{0\}$; hence $\dim U=2k$.
For a codimension-one subspace $R'\le R$, put $L'=U\oplus R'$ and
$S'=(L')^\perp$. Then $\operatorname{rad}(L')=R'$ and
\[
 L'\setminus R'\subseteq L\setminus R,
 \qquad \dim S'=s+1,\qquad \dim R'=j-1.
\]
The inclusion gives $d'\ge d$, while the parameter formulas give
$c'=((s+1)-(j-1))/2=c+1$ and $k'=n-(s+1)+c'=k$.
The ambient space is unchanged, so the length remains $n$.
\end{proof}
Repeating the operation gives distance at least $d$ for every
$c'\in\{c,\ldots,n-k\}$. Conversely, nonexistence at cost $c$ and
distance at least $d$ excludes every smaller cost at the same $(n,k)$:
any such code could be lifted to cost $c$. This justifies the upward
construction propagation and downward exclusion propagation in
Figure~\ref{fig:map}. For the qutrit family, one step reduces the radical
dimension from three to two and raises $c=n-4$ to $n-3$. The 25 open
reference cells have $n=12,\ldots,36$, all within Theorem~\ref{thm:unified}'s
range; their listed upper bound $n-1$ makes each lifted lower bound exact.

\subsection{The closed form, and why it works}\label{app:theorem}
Early evolved constructions $[[9,1,8;6]]_2$ and $[[11,1,10;8]]_2$
(Appendix~\ref{app:campaign}) motivated the odd-length qubit family
recorded in later tasks. The construction below generalizes it to prime
powers. Its algebraic properties have also been independently rechecked
with the Lean kernel; Appendix~\ref{app:lean} gives the formal goal,
proof and correspondence with this construction.

Fix a prime power $q=p^e$, a length $n\ge 2q+1$, and an enumeration
$u_1,\dots,u_q$ of $\F_q$. On qudits $1,\dots,2q$ take $q$ disjoint pairs
and let $\vct{\rho}_i$ be $Z$-type on pair $i$ with $z$-values $(1,-1)$.
Juxtaposition and powers denote concatenation of coordinate pairs;
$(0,1)^0$ is empty, so the tail at $n=2q+1$ contains only $(0,\lambda)$. Put
\[
  \vct{a}=(1,0)^{n},\qquad
  \vct{b}=(u_1,1)(u_1,z_2)\,\big|\cdots\big|\,(u_q,1)(u_q,z_2)\,\big|\,
    (0,1)^{n-2q-1}(0,\lambda),
\]
with $z_2=1$ for odd $p$ and $z_2=0$ for $p=2$, and
$L=\operatorname{span}\{\vct{a},\vct{b},\vct{\rho}_1,\dots,\vct{\rho}_q\}$.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:unified}
Let $q$ be a prime power. For $q\ge3$ with $n\ge2q+1$, or $q=2$
with odd $n\ge5$, one can choose $\lambda\in\F_q^{\times}$ so that
$L$ is the normalizer side of an $[[n,1,n-1;n-q-1]]_q$ EAQECC.
For $q\ge3$, its entanglement cost $c$ is $q-2$ shared pairs below
the threshold in the BDH expression; for $q=2$, it meets that threshold.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The two vectors $\vct{a},\vct{b}\in\F_q^{2n}$ and the $q$ radical
candidates $\vct{\rho}_1,\ldots,\vct{\rho}_q$ are defined above.
Write $R_0=\operatorname{span}\{\vct{\rho}_1,\ldots,\vct{\rho}_q\}$.
Each $\vct{\rho}_i$ has two nonzero $Z$ components on a distinct
pair of qudits, so these $q$ vectors are linearly independent.
Moreover, $\vct{a}$ and $\vct{b}$ have equal $X$ components within
each pair. Their symplectic products with $\vct{\rho}_i$ therefore
vanish. The radical candidates are mutually orthogonal, since they
are all $Z$-type.

The symplectic product of the two vectors is the sum of the $Z$
components of $\vct{b}$:
\[
 \delta:=\langle\vct{a},\vct{b}\rangle
 =q(1+z_2)+(n-2q-1)\,1_{\F_q}+\lambda
 =(n-1)\,1_{\F_q}+\lambda.
\]
Here integers are interpreted in $\F_q$, and $q\,1_{\F_q}=0$
because its characteristic divides $q$. If $q\ge3$, at most one of
the $q-1\ge2$ nonzero choices of $\lambda$ makes $\delta=0$;
choose any other nonzero value. If $q=2$ and $n$ is odd, the only
choice $\lambda=1$ gives $\delta=1$. For even binary $n$, it gives
$\delta=0$: this particular construction loses its nondegenerate pair.
Thus $\delta\ne0$ in every case covered by the theorem.

To establish independence of the full generating set, suppose
$\alpha\vct{a}+\beta\vct{b}+\sum_i t_i\vct{\rho}_i=\vct{0}$.
Since $n\ge2q+1$, the final qudit is outside the support of every
$\vct{\rho}_i$. Its two components are $(\alpha,\lambda\beta)$,
so $\alpha=\beta=0$. Independence of the radical candidates then
implies $t_i=0$ for every $i$. Consequently $\dim L=q+2$.
The restriction of the symplectic form to $L$, in this basis, has
one nondegenerate $2\times2$ block
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&\delta\\-\delta&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
and a $q\times q$ zero block. Hence its rank is two and
$\operatorname{rad}(L)=R_0$.

Every vector outside this radical has the unique form
\[
 \vct{w}=\alpha\vct{a}+\beta\vct{b}
       +\sum_{i=1}^q t_i\vct{\rho}_i,
 \qquad (\alpha,\beta)\ne(0,0).
\]
All $n-2q$ tail qudits are nonzero: their components are
$(\alpha,\beta)$, except for the final $(\alpha,\lambda\beta)$.
On pair $i$, the two coordinate pairs are
\[
 (\alpha+\beta u_i,\,\beta+t_i),\qquad
 (\alpha+\beta u_i,\,\beta z_2-t_i).
\]
If $\beta=0$, then $\alpha\ne0$ and all $n$ qudits are nonzero.
If $\beta\ne0$, exactly one index $i$ satisfies
$u_i=-\alpha/\beta$, since $u_1,\ldots,u_q$ enumerate all $q$ elements of the finite field $\F_q$,
and $-\alpha/\beta\in\F_q$.
Every other pair has two nonzero $X$ components. On the exceptional
pair, both $Z$ components could vanish only if
$t_i=-\beta=\beta z_2$. This is impossible because
$1+z_2\ne0$: it equals $2$ in odd characteristic and $1$ in
characteristic two. At most one qudit can therefore be zero, giving
$\wt(\vct{w})\ge n-1$.

This lower bound is attained. Fix any index $i$, choose
$\beta=1$, $\alpha=-u_i$, $t_i=-1$, and $t_h=0$ for $h\ne i$.
The first qudit of pair $i$ is zero and its second has nonzero
$Z$ component $1+z_2$. All other pairs have nonzero $X$ components,
and the tail remains nonzero. This vector lies outside $R_0$ and
has weight exactly $n-1$.

Finally, set $S=L^\perp$. Nondegeneracy of the ambient symplectic
form gives $S^\perp=L$ and $\dim S=2n-q-2$. Also
$S\cap S^\perp=L^\perp\cap L=R_0$, whose dimension is $q$.
The parameter formulas in Appendix~\ref{app:symplectic} now give
\[
 c=\frac{2n-q-2-q}{2}=n-q-1,\qquad
 k=n-(2n-q-2)+c=1,\qquad d=n-1.
\]
For these parameters,
$2(d-1)-(n-k+c)=(2n-4)-(2n-q-2)=q-2$.
Thus the BDH threshold exceeds $c$ by $q-2$ shared pairs for $q\ge3$
and met with equality for $q=2$, as stated.
\end{proof}
The auditor instantiates this construction and verifies every member
exactly for $q\in\{2,3,4,5\}$ and $2q+1\le n\le 2q+8$ (claim
\claim{families}); the theorem covers every prime power.

\subsection{Reference bounds and target selection}\label{app:tables}
The reference tables record, for each parameter cell $(q,n,k,c)$,
the listed lower and upper distance bounds and their construction sources.
Our comparison uses the bounds accessed on 17 July 2026. The reference
qubit table contains 563 entries whose distance bounds differ by one. The artifact
preserves these table data and a second copy retrieved on 10 September
2026; both cover qubit codes with $n\le64$ and qutrit codes with $n\le36$.
The reproduction command uses the July reference data unless another
retrieval date is explicitly selected. Schema checks reject malformed
records before classifying improvements or previously unlisted cells.

An entry with distance interval $[d_\ell,d_u]$ closes when construction
and nonexistence bounds meet. For $(q,n,k,c)=(2,7,1,4)$, a verified
$[[7,1,6;4]]_2$ code closes the listed $5\le d\le6$ gap.
Cells absent from the reference tables are called \emph{record targets} in the
archive; absence alone does not establish novelty relative to all
published constructions.

The familiar BDH Singleton expression $2(d-1)\le n-k+c$ is not a
general bound in the large-distance regime~\citep{Grassl2021}. Our
principal family has $d=n-1$, so we use the applicable generalized
Singleton bounds of \citet{GHW2022}. The $[[12,1,11;5]]_5$ code,
for example, uses four fewer pairs than substitution into the BDH
expression would suggest; it does not contradict a valid upper bound.

For qubit ($q=2$) linear stabilizer EAQECCs with $k\ge1$, the
EA-Plotkin bound~\citep{GuoLi2013} gives
$d\le\lfloor3\cdot4^{k-1}n/(4^k-1)\rfloor$.
It lowers 398 upper bounds in the reference \emph{qubit} table.
The qubit target list retains cells not excluded by this inequality,
the applicable Singleton bounds, or our certified refutations.

\subsection{Campaign configuration and verification}\label{app:campaign}
Across the four discovery campaigns, Google Cloud Vertex AI's
AlphaEvolve service sampled
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} and \texttt{gemini-3.1-pro-preview} equally,
with four generation and four evaluation workers. The four campaigns
requested $120$, $120$, $80$ and $80$ programs; worker logs record
$120$, $121$, $80$ and $80$ completed searches (401 total). Service
counters are $121$, $122$, $81$ and $81$, each including an initial
program with a supplied score. Completed searches determine the nominal
allowance: $401\times240$\,s, or $26.73$\,h. Archived records report
$1.87$\,M input and $3.74$\,M output tokens. Campaigns 1--2 share one
task specification and campaigns 3--4 another (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

Campaign 1 records 120 completed searches. The first new
$[[9,1,8;6]]_2$ and $[[11,1,10;8]]_2$ codes precede completions 4
and 119, respectively: 0.267\,h and 7.933\,h of allocated
search budget at 240\,s per completed search. The logs do not provide
elapsed discovery times. A later task records $[[7,1,6;4]]_2$ as an
earlier construction without a first-hit budget. The $[[13,1,12;10]]_2$
record instead cites the closed-form theorem as its source.
Initial and later tasks document the transition from finite constructions
to the researcher-derived family. Algorithm~\ref{alg:search} is a later
retained program; the logs do not identify a surviving source for every
early discovery. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} uses separately generated,
preserved programs and its own protocol.

For the complementary finite-field searches at $q=4,5$,
simulated annealing searches\label{app:nonbinary-search}
$L=\operatorname{span}\{\vct\rho_1,\ldots,\vct\rho_j,\vct a,\vct b\}$
with $j=n-1-c$ fixed, disjoint $Z$-pair rows, each with entries $(1,1)$.
Moves resample up to three entries of $\vct a$ or $\vct b$.
The objective counts non-radical span vectors below distance $n-1$ by
exact enumeration and Gram tests. Full-rank and
$\langle\vct a,\vct b\rangle_s\ne0$ checks prune proposals; Metropolis acceptance, cooling and restarts guide search.
Before saving, a separate parameter calculation checks $(k,c,d)=(1,c,n-1)$.
All 23 archived matrices have this form and pass the independent Magma
check. The \artifactlink{} supplies the routines and code-to-record mappings.

The independent Magma check receives generators only and re-derives every quantity we
report. Symplectic weight is obtained by embedding
$(x_i,z_i)\mapsto x_i+\omega z_i$ into $\mathrm{GF}(q^2)$, choosing
$\omega\notin\F_q$. Then $\{1,\omega\}$ is an $\F_q$-basis, so the image
is zero exactly when $(x_i,z_i)=(0,0)$. Magma's own \texttt{Weight()}
therefore counts nonzero coordinate pairs from the supplied generators.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:search} summarizes the archived evolved construction
program; its preserved source is available in the
\artifactlink{}.\label{app:construction-program}
The generator matrix $\mathbf{G}$ spans $S$, and $best[t]$ stores the
lowest-cost proposal seen for target $t$. The objective remains
$5000|r.c-c_t|+1000|r.k-k_t|+r.\mathrm{offending}$, with cost $10^9$
for an evaluator error. Here $r.\mathrm{offending}$ counts
$\vct v\in S^\perp\setminus S_{\mathrm{iso}}$ with $\wt(\vct v)<d_t$;
it is an integer count. After return, the external evaluator instead
maximizes $1000-\mathrm{offending}$ for matching $(k,c)$, adding $10^6$
for a newly achieved archive target. A signature mismatch scores
$-5000|r.c-c_t|-1000$; the best proposal sets the program score.
Both calls use the exact parameter routine; Magma supplies the separate
implementation check.
The campaign seed used fixed target weights
$n_t^{-2}$. The evolved program uses normalized weights proportional to
$n_t^{-1.5}(1+10b_t)$, initially with $b_t=0$. After a repair attempt,
\textsc{ProgressBonus} uses the stored best cost $o_t^\star$:
\[
 b_t\gets
 \begin{cases}
  (1+o_t^\star)^{-1},&0<o_t^\star<100,\\
  0,&o_t^\star=0,\\
  b_t,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
If no proposal was recorded, take $o_t^\star=10^9$. A proposal with
zero cost at its initial evaluation takes the earlier \textbf{continue}
branch and skips this bonus update, exactly as in the source.

For a new attempt, the program samples one scalar $u$ and tests branches
in order. If $u<0.2$ and a stored positive-cost proposal exists, it resumes
from that matrix. Otherwise, \textsc{Construct} uses a cyclic construction
when $u<0.4$ and $s\le2n$, a divisor-stride construction when $u<0.6$
and $s\le2n$, or a block construction when $u<0.8$. If none applies,
or the resulting rank is not $s$, it uses a random stabilizer.
The construction branches and their validity checks remain subject to the
same exact evaluator as the seed.

The cyclic branches use one or two nonzero base vectors. Row $i$ takes
base $i\bmod m$ and rotates it by
$2h\lfloor i/m\rfloor\bmod 2n$ bits, where $m$ is the number of bases.
The ordinary cyclic branch sets $h=1$; the added branch samples a positive
proper divisor of $n$. The block branch extends the allowed dual dimension
from 8 to 12 and block sizes from $\{2,4,6\}$ to $\{2,4,6,8\}$.

During repair, \textsc{MixedMove} samples $v\in[0,1)$: if $v<0.2$
and $s\ge2$, XOR two distinct rows; otherwise, if $v<0.4$, rotate one
row by $1,\ldots,n-1$ qubits uniformly; otherwise, apply a weight-1--3
Pauli perturbation to one row. The rotation changes one generator,
not all coordinates of the code. Rank-deficient proposals increment $f$
without evaluation. The inherited Metropolis loop cools by $0.995$ to
$0.1$ and restarts after 1,500 attempts without an accepted strict
improvement. It retains each target's best accepted proposal and returns
at most 16. Figure~\ref{fig:lineage} shows source changes, not their
individual performance effects.

\subsection{The refutation encoding}\label{app:sat}
Encode $L=S^\perp$ using $j=n-k-c$ radical generators
$\vct\rho_1,\ldots,\vct\rho_j$ and $k$ hyperbolic pairs
$(\vct u_i,\vct v_i)$. Gram constraints enforce rank $2k$ and pair
independence. For qubits, all $2^{2k+j}-2^j$ non-radical combinations
must have weight at least $d$. Gray-code chaining changes one generator
contribution at a time; Sinz counters enforce weight. When $j\ge1$,
local symplectic maps and coordinate permutations make $\vct\rho_1$
a nonzero $Z$-prefix. For its bits $(x_i,z_i)$, the CNF sets
$x_i=0$, $z_1=1$ and adds $(z_i\lor\neg z_{i+1})$ for $i<n$.
These clauses allow every nonempty prefix in one CNF, without fixing its
weight or making the other radical rows $Z$-only.

For qutrits, each field element is one-hot encoded. The radical basis is
in reduced row echelon form, and modular state chains impose its
Gram constraints. For $k=1$, it suffices to test the four projective
logical representatives $\vct{u},\vct{v},\vct{u}+\vct{v},\vct{u}+2\vct{v}$, each shifted by every radical
vector. Their nonzero scalar multiples have the same weights. At
$d=n-1$, pairwise clauses prevent a representative from vanishing at two
coordinates. The following lemma permits an additional normalization.

\begin{lemma}[Coordinate normalization]\label{lem:coordinate-normalization}
Let $L\le\F_q^{2n}$ have radical $R$ with $\dim L=\dim R+2$ and
$\dim R>2$. If every vector in $L\setminus R$ has symplectic weight at
least $n-1$, then local symplectic transformations can make $R$ entirely
$Z$-type.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
By definition, $R=\operatorname{rad}(L)=L\cap L^\perp$.
Let $\pi_i:\F_q^{2n}\to\F_q^2$ be $\pi_i(\vct v)=(x_i,z_i)$,
and $\pi_{ih}=(\pi_i,\pi_h):\F_q^{2n}\to\F_q^4$ for $i\ne h$.
Writing $K_{ih}=\ker(\pi_{ih}|_L)$, the distance hypothesis gives
\[
 K_{ih}\subseteq\{\vct v\in L:\wt(\vct v)\le n-2\}\subseteq R,
 \qquad K_{ih}=\ker(\pi_{ih}|_R).
\]
Rank--nullity therefore yields
\[
 \dim\pi_{ih}(L)-\dim\pi_{ih}(R)=\dim L-\dim R=2,
 \qquad \dim\pi_{ih}(R)\le2.
\]
Suppose $\dim\pi_i(R)=2$. For every $h\ne i$,
$2=\dim\pi_i(R)\le\dim\pi_{ih}(R)\le2$. Hence the inclusion
$\ker(\pi_{ih}|_R)\subseteq\ker(\pi_i|_R)$ is an equality, since
both kernels have dimension $\dim R-2$. Consequently
\[
 \ker(\pi_i|_R)=\bigcap_{h\ne i}\ker(\pi_{ih}|_R)=\{\vct0\},
\]
which implies $\dim R=\dim\pi_i(R)=2$, a contradiction.
Thus $\dim P_i\le1$ for every $P_i:=\pi_i(R)$.

If $P_i=\{\vct0\}$, take $M_i=I_2$. Otherwise write
$P_i=\operatorname{span}\{\vct w_i\}$ with
$\vct w_i=(a_i,b_i)^{\mathsf T}\ne\vct0$, and set
\[
 M_i=
 \begin{cases}
  \begin{pmatrix}b_i&-a_i\\a_i^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},&a_i\ne0,\\[3pt]
  \begin{pmatrix}b_i&0\\0&b_i^{-1}\end{pmatrix},&a_i=0.
 \end{cases}
\]
In both cases $\det M_i=1$ and $M_i\vct w_i=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
For $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$M_i^{\mathsf T}JM_i=J$. Therefore
$T=\operatorname{diag}(M_1,\ldots,M_n)$ is symplectic and
\[
 T(R)\subseteq\{(0,z_1,\ldots,0,z_n):z_i\in\F_q\},
 \qquad \wt(T\vct v)=\wt(\vct v)\quad(\vct v\in\F_q^{2n}).
\]
The weight identity follows because every $M_i$ is invertible.
Thus $T$ gives the required local normalization.
\end{proof}

The lemma also applies to binary targets with $k=1$, $d\ge n-1$
and $j>2$. Adding all radical-$X$ zero constraints to the seven
binary exclusions gives seven further UNSAT instances with checked
DRAT proofs. A feasible $[[6,1,5;2]]_2$ control remains SAT and its
decoded generators pass independent parameter checks. The artifact
preserves these normalized certificates alongside the original ones.

For $[[10,1,9;4]]_3$, $j=5$. Row reduction preserves the zero
$X$ components, so the normalized encoding retains every possible code.
Adding $jn=50$ unit clauses gives $33{,}033$ variables and $476{,}271$ clauses.
The lemma establishes completeness; DRAT certifies unsatisfiability.
As a positive control, the same normalized encoder is SAT when fixed to
the archived $[[10,1,9;5]]_3$ code. The \artifactlink{} supplies the CNF,
proof, normalization metadata and checks of the added clauses.
